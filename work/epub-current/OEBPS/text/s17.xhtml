ORIGINAL STORY TWO

Mulenga and the Flooded Footpath

Heavy rain drummed on the roofs throughout the night. It filled water drums, bent the tall grass and turned the dusty paths into ribbons of mud.

By morning, the stream beside Mulenga's village had grown wide and brown. Its usual gentle sound had become a deep, rushing roar.

Mulenga woke early because his class was presenting science projects. He had spent two weeks building a model bridge from thin sticks, string and carefully folded cardboard.

He wrapped the model in a plastic sack and held it against his chest.

At the mango tree, Jelita waited in her yellow blouse and navy skirt. She studied the dark clouds.

"My grandmother says the lower footpath floods after rain like this," she warned.

"We cannot be late," Mulenga replied. "My presentation is first, and everyone is expecting to see my bridge."

"A bridge model is not more important than a safe journey," Jelita said.

Mulenga did not answer. He was certain the stepping stones would still be visible.

The Dangerous Crossing

When they reached the stream, the water was already flowing over the footpath. Only the tops of two stepping stones could be seen.

A broken branch spun in the current, struck a rock and disappeared downstream.

Mulenga tested the mud with one shoe. "If we move quickly, we can cross before the water rises further."

Jelita caught his sleeve. "Look at that branch. The current carried it away as if it weighed nothing."

Two younger pupils, Chipo and Temwani, arrived behind them. They were nervous, but when they saw Mulenga near the water, they stepped closer.

Mulenga realised that the younger children often copied him. If he attempted the crossing, they might follow even if he told them not to.

He remembered his father's advice: A leader's actions speak before his words.

Mulenga stepped away from the edge. "We are not crossing here," he announced. "We will use the upper road."

The upper road curved around a hill and added nearly an hour to the journey. Mulenga worried about his presentation, but the angry water had made the correct choice clear.

Warning the Community

Before leaving, Jelita removed a bright red cloth from the top of her lunch basket. Mulenga tied it between two trees across the approach to the path.

They added branches beneath it so that anyone coming from the village would stop before reaching the flooded bank.

"We should tell an adult," Chipo said.

On the upper road, they met Mr Banda, a farmer guiding an ox-cart loaded with empty grain sacks.

He listened carefully when the children described the flooded crossing.

"You made a wise decision," he said. "Climb onto the cart, little ones. I will take you as far as the school junction, then alert the headman."

Jelita and Mulenga walked beside the cart because their muddy shoes would have dirtied the sacks. They helped push when one wheel sank into soft ground.

The journey was slow, but no one complained.

The Strongest Bridge

They arrived after the first lesson. Mulenga expected the teacher to be angry, but news of the flood had already reached the school.

Several pupils from other villages were also late. The presentations had been postponed until everyone arrived safely.

When Mulenga's turn came, he placed his model on the table. He explained how triangles spread weight and how strong supports protect a bridge.

Then he paused and told the class what had happened at the stream.

"My model stayed dry," he said, ?but I learned something more important than the facts in my presentation. A strong bridge helps people cross, but good judgement keeps them away when there is no safe bridge."

The class discussed how to warn families during floods. They painted signs showing a crossed-out stream and arrows pointing toward the upper road.

Community members inspected the crossing that weekend. They cleared blocked drainage channels and began planning a raised footbridge above the flood level.

At assembly, Mulenga received a special safety certificate. He felt proud, but not because he had won a prize.

He was proud because Chipo and Temwani had reached school safely.

On the way home, Jelita nudged him. "You were right about one thing."

"What was that?"

"Your bridge project was important. It helped the whole community think about building a real one."

Mulenga smiled. He had discovered that courage did not always mean moving forward. Sometimes courage meant stopping, thinking and choosing a safer direction.

Lesson from the Story

Real courage means choosing safety and protecting others.

New Words

  • Current: water moving in a particular direction
  • Risk: the possibility of danger or harm
  • Responsible: careful and dependable
  • Judgement: the ability to make a sensible decision

Knowledge Check / Think About It

1. Why did Mulenga first want to cross the stream?

A. He wanted to swim.

B. He lost his shoes.

C. He wanted to reach his science presentation.

Answer: C

2. Why did he finally choose the upper road?

A. The current was dangerous and younger pupils might copy him.

B. The upper road was shorter.

C. School was cancelled.

Answer: A

3. What did the children do to warn others?

A. They removed the stones.

B. They marked the path with bright cloth.

C. They told nobody.

Answer: B

4. What is the safest response to a flooded crossing?

A. Cross quickly.

B. Ask a younger child to test it.

C. Turn back and use a safe route.

Answer: C
