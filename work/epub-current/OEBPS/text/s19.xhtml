ORIGINAL STORY FOUR

Jelita and Mulenga at the Science Fair

A colourful poster appeared beside the head teacher's office: DISTRICT SCIENCE FAIR?SIMPLE SOLUTIONS FOR EVERYDAY PROBLEMS.

Jelita read it twice. She had always wondered whether sunlight could preserve fruit without an expensive electric machine.

Mulenga wanted to build an alarm that warned families when a water container was nearly empty.

Their teacher, Mr Zulu, liked both ideas. Unfortunately, the school could send only one project team and provide one display table.

"Work together," he suggested. "A good team can sometimes discover a solution that neither person would find alone."

At their first meeting, Jelita drew plans for a solar food dryer. Mulenga pushed her paper aside to demonstrate a bottle, string and bell.

At their second meeting, Mulenga built a wooden frame while Jelita covered another box with clear plastic.

After a week, they had two unfinished models and very little cooperation.

Jelita's grandmother found them arguing on the veranda.

She listened quietly, then asked, "Whose problem are you solving?"

Neither child could answer.

Listening Before Building

The next day, they visited families, the school kitchen and market traders.

A fruit seller explained that ripe bananas and mangoes sometimes spoiled before they could be sold, especially when rain kept customers away.

The school cook said fruit had to be washed carefully, but the cleaning container sometimes ran empty during busy mornings.

A farmer added that an invention would be most useful if local people could repair it with affordable materials.

Jelita filled several notebook pages. Mulenga stopped talking about which idea was better and began asking questions.

At the market, a trader named Auntie Ruth examined their drawings.

"Why must the dryer and water indicator be separate?" she asked. "Clean water is part of preparing safe food."

On the walk home, Jelita and Mulenga saw the connection.

They designed a solar dryer made from a wooden box, a clear sloping cover and screened trays. A small washable water container stood beside it with a floating marker that showed the water level.

The First Failure

Their first test took place on a bright Saturday. They washed banana slices, arranged them on the trays and placed the dryer in direct sunlight.

The inside became warm, but by afternoon the fruit was still damp. Drops of water covered the clear lid.

Mulenga looked around nervously. "We should not mention this test to the judges."

Jelita wrote FAILED?MOISTURE TRAPPED in their notebook.

"If we hide what went wrong, we will repeat it," she said. "Failure is information."

They asked Mr Zulu why the moisture remained inside. He did not give them the answer. He asked them to think about where damp air could escape.

They added screened vents near the bottom and top of the box. Warm air rose through the upper vent while fresh air entered below.

The second batch dried better, but slowly. They raised the trays and changed the angle of the clear cover to catch more sunlight.

They tested equal pieces of fruit, recorded drying times and asked Auntie Ruth to examine the final results.

"This batch is evenly dried," she said. "And insects cannot reach it through the screen."

A Difficult Morning

On the morning of the fair, clouds covered the sky.

Mulenga worried that their solar dryer would appear useless. Jelita packed samples from earlier tests, their notebook and a small lamp that demonstrated how light entered the box.

At the exhibition hall, another team displayed a polished electric dryer with switches and bright lights.

The Jelita and Mulenga model looked simple beside it. Its wood showed pencil marks, and one corner had been repaired twice.

"Ours looks ordinary," Mulenga whispered.

"It is made from materials people can find and repair," Jelita replied. "That is part of our solution."

Sharing the Discovery

When the judges arrived, Mulenga explained the problem reported by families and traders.

Jelita showed the first failed test, the trapped moisture in their drawing and every change they had made.

They demonstrated the floating water marker and explained why washing fruit and hands was important.

One judge asked, "What would you improve next?"

Mulenga suggested a stronger adjustable stand. Jelita proposed testing different screens and adding a simple rain cover.

The judges were impressed that the pupils understood their project's limitations instead of pretending it was perfect.

At the closing ceremony, Jelita and Mulenga received the Practical Innovation Award.

More importantly, Auntie Ruth and another trader asked the environmental club to help build larger dryers for the market.

On the bus home, Mulenga turned the certificate over in his hands.

"Your idea did not defeat mine," he said.

"And yours did not defeat mine," Jelita replied. "They became more useful together."

They opened a fresh notebook. On the first page they wrote a new question: What other everyday problem could be solved by listening first?

Lesson from the Story

Good inventions grow from listening, testing and learning from failure.

New Words

  • Solution: an answer to a problem
  • Prototype: an early model used for testing
  • Evidence: information that helps show what is true
  • Innovation: a useful new idea or method

Knowledge Check / Think About It

1. Why did Jelita and Mulenga make little progress at first?

A. They had too many models.

B. They competed instead of sharing a problem.

C. The fair was cancelled.

Answer: B

2. How did community interviews improve their project?

A. They avoided testing.

B. They focused only on appearance.

C. They discovered real needs and combined ideas.

Answer: C

3. Why did Jelita record the failed test?

A. Failure provided evidence for improvement.

B. She wanted to lose.

C. The test was perfect.

Answer: A

4. Which process is most likely to produce a useful invention?

A. Build without understanding.

B. Listen, build, test and improve.

C. Hide every failure.

Answer: B
