CHILDREN OF THE NORTHWEST THREE

Chawana and the Injured Crowned Crane

Chawana was the youngest member of the school nature club and the first to arrive whenever an animal needed help.

One afternoon, she heard a weak flutter near a wet field beyond the garden.

A crowned crane stood among the reeds. One wing hung lower than the other, and a length of discarded string was tangled around its leg.

Chawana moved closer with both hands open.

"Wait," Wana said. "A frightened wild bird may injure itself or you."

"But we cannot leave it," Chawana replied.

"We will help wisely," Chisola said, "not hurriedly."

Help from Knowledgeable Adults

The children kept a safe distance and asked Chiwape to call Mrs Kankasa.

They stopped people and dogs from approaching, observed the bird's location and placed no food or water near it.

Mrs Kankasa contacted a local wildlife officer. While they waited, she explained that wild animals may have special diets, hidden injuries or diseases that untrained people cannot recognise.

Chawana worried that waiting meant doing nothing.

"Protecting the space is an important action," Mrs Kankasa assured her.

The officer arrived with a ventilated carrier, a cloth and protective gloves. He approached slowly, covered the crane to calm it and removed the string.

The wing was bruised rather than broken. The bird needed quiet observation before release.

The String Problem

The officer showed the children the tangled line. It had probably come from a torn sack or discarded parcel.

"Small waste can become a dangerous trap," he said.

Chawana remembered seeing loose string near the market and football field.

The club organised a safe litter survey. They used tools and gloves, and adults handled anything sharp or unknown.

Vumbi and Chiwape made collection frames for reusable cord. Bernice prepared a short assembly talk about securing string, fishing line and plastic loops before disposal.

Chishomyo designed covered collection boxes that prevented wind from carrying light waste away.

Three days later, the children watched from a distance as the recovered crane was released near the wetland.

It stepped cautiously, opened both wings and rose above the reeds.

Chawana wanted to run after it, but she remained still and let the bird choose its direction.

"I thought kindness meant touching it and making it better myself," she said.

Wana smiled. "Sometimes kindness means finding the person who knows how to help."

Chawana kept one grey feather the officer found in the empty carrier. She placed it beside the club's litter map as a reminder that compassion works best when guided by knowledge.

Lesson from the Story

Helping wildlife safely means keeping a distance and contacting knowledgeable adults.

New Words

  • Wildlife: animals and plants living naturally in an area
  • Tangled: twisted together so that movement is difficult
  • Ventilated: designed to allow fresh air to enter
  • Compassion: concern for someone or something that is suffering

Knowledge Check / Think About It

1. Why did Wana stop Chawana from approaching the crane?

A. The bird was asleep.

B. A frightened wild bird could injure itself or her.

C. Chawana disliked cranes.

Answer: B

2. What useful action did the children take while waiting?

A. They chased the crane.

B. They fed it unknown food.

C. They protected the area and contacted an adult.

Answer: C

3. What had injured the crane?

A. Discarded string tangled around its leg.

B. A tree branch broke its wing.

C. The children touched it.

Answer: A

4. What is the safest response to an injured wild animal?

A. Take it home immediately.

B. Keep a safe distance and contact trained adults.

C. Give it human medicine.

Answer: B
