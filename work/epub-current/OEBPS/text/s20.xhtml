CHILDREN OF THE NORTHWEST ONE

Chisola and the Secret of Kifubwa Stream

Chisola noticed small details that other people often passed without seeing.

She noticed when the first mushrooms appeared after rain, when ants moved their eggs before a storm and when the Kifubwa Stream changed colour after a busy weekend.

One Monday, the water below the footbridge carried plastic wrappers and an oily rainbow shimmer.

"The stream looks unwell," Chisola told her friends at school.

Vumbi shrugged. "Perhaps yesterday's rain washed soil into it."

Bernice looked carefully. "Soil does not usually shine like that."

Their environmental-club teacher, Mrs Kankasa, warned them not to touch or taste unknown water. She gave them gloves, observation sheets and permission to investigate from safe public paths with an adult.

Looking for Evidence

On Saturday, Chisola, Bernice, Vumbi, Wana, Chishomyo, Chiwape and young Chawana followed Mrs Kankasa along the stream.

They recorded what they saw without accusing anyone. Upstream, the water was clear. Near the market path, wrappers had collected against reeds. Farther down, rainwater flowed from a blocked roadside drain.

Chishomyo sketched a map. Wana counted pieces of litter in three equal areas. Bernice interviewed willing traders while Mrs Kankasa listened.

Most traders used bins, but strong wind scattered rubbish from an uncovered collection point. A mechanic showed them a leaking container that had already been moved away from the drain.

Vumbi wanted to announce that they had found the guilty person.

"We have found several causes, not one villain," Chisola said. "We must report what we observed accurately."

A Community Solution

The children presented their notes to the market committee and local council representative.

They proposed covered bins, a regular collection day, drain clearing and signs explaining why waste should never enter the stream.

Adults handled the oily container and any unsafe material. The children wore gloves and collected ordinary litter from dry ground.

Chawana painted a blue stream on the first sign. Bernice added fish and reeds. Chiwape made everyone laugh by drawing a wrapper with running legs.

After the next rain, water moved freely through the cleared drain. The oily shine disappeared, although Chisola knew the stream would need continued care.

The club created a monthly observation walk. They compared photographs, counted litter and reported problems to adults.

At assembly, Chisola explained that protecting nature was not a one-day performance.

"The stream told us something was wrong," she said. "We helped because we looked carefully, gathered evidence and worked with the community."

From then on, whenever her friends hurried over the bridge, they remembered to pause and look at the water below.

Lesson from the Story

Protecting nature begins with careful observation, reliable evidence and responsible action.

New Words

  • Observe: to watch something carefully
  • Evidence: information that helps establish what happened
  • Contamination: the presence of something harmful or unwanted
  • Responsible: acting carefully and accepting a duty

Knowledge Check / Think About It

1. What first made Chisola concerned about the stream?

A. The bridge disappeared.

B. The stream became completely dry.

C. An oily shine and litter appeared in the water.

Answer: C

2. Why did the children investigate with an adult?

A. Unknown water and waste could be unsafe.

B. They wanted to miss school.

C. Adults could not observe anything.

Answer: A

3. Why did Chisola refuse to blame one person?

A. She did not care about the stream.

B. The evidence showed several contributing causes.

C. Nobody had used the market.

Answer: B

4. Which action offers the best long-term protection for the stream?

A. One clean-up with no follow-up.

B. Moving litter farther downstream.

C. Regular monitoring, covered bins and clear drains.

Answer: C
