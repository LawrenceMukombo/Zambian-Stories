CHILDREN OF THE NORTHWEST FIVE

Wana and the Kabompo River Crossing

Wana rarely spoke first, but when he did, his friends listened.

The school culture club planned to visit a neighbouring community across the Kabompo River for a day of crafts, music and shared games.

Adults arranged a registered boat operator, life jackets and a safe landing point.

During the night, rain fell far upstream. By morning, the river was higher and faster than expected.

Some pupils had already gathered at the bank when Wana noticed branches moving swiftly past the landing.

"The river has changed since yesterday," he told the teacher.

The operator inspected the water and postponed the crossing.

"But the other school is waiting!" Vumbi protested.

"Waiting is better than searching for us," Wana replied.

A Celebration Without the Crossing

Bernice suggested cancelling everything. Chawana looked close to tears because she had prepared a welcome picture.

Wana studied the club schedule. The visiting school had a teacher with a mobile telephone, and both communities had meeting shelters.

"Could we hold two connected celebrations today and meet in person later?" he asked.

Chishomyo and Chiwape helped position a borrowed speaker and telephone where groups could exchange songs and greetings.

Bernice presented her story from their side of the river. The distant group answered with riddles and applause that crackled through the speaker.

Children displayed baskets, drawings and small models to the camera. Chawana held her welcome picture high enough for everyone to see.

The arrangement was imperfect, but the celebration continued without risking the crossing.

Reading the River

Later, the boat operator taught the club how professionals assess changing conditions.

He explained that clear weather at one place does not guarantee calm water because rain may have fallen far upstream.

A familiar river can still become dangerous. Life jackets, trained operators and adult decisions remain necessary even for short journeys.

Wana asked questions and recorded the answers for a water-safety poster.

Two days later, the river level dropped and authorised adults confirmed safe conditions.

The clubs finally met. Before boarding, every child wore a correctly fitted life jacket and listened to the operator's instructions.

Vumbi joked that Wana had turned waiting into an entire festival.

"Waiting gave us another story to tell," Bernice said.

At the opposite bank, pupils welcomed them with clapping and laughter.

Wana understood that calm judgement did not stop good experiences. It helped make sure everyone lived to enjoy them.

The clubs agreed to include a weather check and an alternative plan in every future journey.

Lesson from the Story

Calm judgement and respect for safety guidance protect the whole community.

New Words

  • Postpone: to arrange for something to happen later
  • Upstream: toward the source of a river
  • Operator: a trained person who controls equipment or transport
  • Alternative: another available choice or plan

Knowledge Check / Think About It

1. What change did Wana notice at the river?

A. The water was higher, faster and carrying branches.

B. The boat had changed colour.

C. The landing moved uphill.

Answer: A

2. Why could the river be dangerous despite clearer local weather?

A. Sunlight always speeds rivers.

B. Rain may have fallen upstream.

C. Life jackets cause fast water.

Answer: B

3. How did the clubs continue their celebration?

A. They crossed without permission.

B. They ended the club permanently.

C. They connected the celebrations by telephone and speaker.

Answer: C

4. What must children do before a supervised boat journey?

A. Wear a fitted life jacket and obey the trained operator.

B. Stand throughout the trip.

C. Choose the fastest boat alone.

Answer: A
