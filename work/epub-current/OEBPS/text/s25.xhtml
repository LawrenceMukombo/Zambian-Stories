CHILDREN OF THE NORTHWEST SIX

Chishomyo, Chiwape and the Water-Saving Machine

The school garden produced healthy vegetables, but carrying water from the tap took time and sometimes wasted more than the plants received.

Chishomyo loved measuring, drawing and improving machines. Chiwape preferred building quickly with whatever materials he could find.

When the science teacher challenged them to reduce water waste, both boys claimed they already had the best solution.

Chishomyo designed a neat network of narrow tubes. Chiwape built a tipping bucket from a container, wire and a wooden stand.

"Mine is precise," Chishomyo said.

"Mine actually moves," Chiwape replied.

They decided to test their inventions on separate garden beds.

Two Unsuccessful Designs

Chishomyo's tubes delivered water evenly at first, but tiny pieces of dirt blocked several holes.

Chiwape's bucket tipped dramatically and made everyone cheer. Unfortunately, most of its water rushed onto one plant and washed soil away from the roots.

Each boy explained why the other design had failed.

Chisola interrupted. "The garden does not need a winner. It needs water."

Bernice suggested writing down what worked in each design.

Chishomyo's system controlled the flow. Chiwape's raised container used gravity and did not require electricity.

Wana measured how much water each bed received. Vumbi timed the flow. Chawana placed small cups at different points to reveal uneven watering.

One Better Machine

The boys combined their ideas. They raised a covered container on Chiwape's strong wooden stand and connected it to Chishomyo's tubes.

They added a simple cloth filter to stop dirt and used adjustable drippers made from cleaned bottle caps.

The first combined test still leaked at one connection. Chiwape tightened it with a reusable rubber strip.

The farthest plants received too little water, so Chishomyo changed the tube height and retested the system.

After several trials, each plant received a slow flow near its roots.

The team compared the amount used with ordinary bucket watering. Their system saved water and reduced the time pupils spent carrying heavy containers.

Sharing the Design

At assembly, Chishomyo explained the measurements while Chiwape demonstrated the working model.

They openly showed the failed tube and tipping bucket.

"These were not useless," Chiwape said. "They taught us what the final design needed."

The school caretaker helped the club build a safer permanent stand. Teachers reminded pupils that adults must supervise tools and elevated water containers.

The environmental club created illustrated instructions so other classes could reproduce the system with suitable local materials.

Chishomyo wrote both names on the design page.

Chiwape added the names of every child who had measured, timed, filtered and tested.

Their machine worked because one boy brought precision, another brought practical building skills and the whole team brought evidence.

As water dripped quietly into the soil, Chisola smiled. The garden had not received a winning invention. It had received a shared one.

Lesson from the Story

Better solutions emerge when people test ideas, learn from failure and combine their strengths.

New Words

  • Precise: accurate and carefully controlled
  • Gravity: the force that pulls objects toward the ground
  • Filter: material or equipment that removes unwanted particles
  • Efficient: working well without wasting time or resources

Knowledge Check / Think About It

1. Why did the two first designs fail?

A. Both worked perfectly.

B. Holes blocked in one and the other washed soil away.

C. Neither contained water.

Answer: B

2. Which strengths were combined in the final machine?

A. Decoration and loud sound.

B. Speed and competition.

C. Precise flow control and a gravity-fed raised container.

Answer: C

3. Why did the group measure water at different points?

A. To check whether plants received water evenly.

B. To choose the tallest pupil.

C. To make the test longer.

Answer: A

4. What is the best response when a prototype fails?

A. Hide the failure.

B. Record the evidence, improve it and test again.

C. Repeat without changes.

Answer: B
